## How he sees the world

Mosquera looks at the underground. Where the surface of Buenos Aires sees a subway
network, he sees a city's unconscious: tunnels where what the country buried keeps
moving in the dark. *Moebius* disguises itself as topological science fiction —a train
swallowed by a network folded into an impossible surface— but its real question is how
a society can lose people and then organize itself not to see the loss. It speaks of
the disappeared without ever naming them, and for that very reason names them better:
the metaphor says what the literal could not.

What interests me about his gaze is that it thinks in Borges's language without quoting
him: the labyrinth that escaped its own blueprint, the rational detective following a
geometric clue into the trap, the order invented by decree that displaces the fact. And
his working method mirrors his subject: he made *Moebius* as a collective workshop with
his students at the Universidad del Cine —a structure bigger than any author, just as
the network is bigger than any passenger. A film made with almost nothing that thinks
better than far larger productions, because its central image *is* its argument.

## Obsessions

- **Absence as a presence that won't dissolve.** What is lost isn't there but hasn't
  left: it keeps running, audible, sensed, in a fold no map admits. The exact grammar
  of the disappeared —neither dead nor alive, erased from the record but not from the
  air.
- **Bureaucracy as the faceless villain.** No torturers on screen: the horror is
  administrative, office men who would rather close the question than answer it. Evil
  is the refusal to look.
- **Mathematics as political figure.** The Möbius strip —one side, no inside or
  outside— as the exact shape of a State that lost the boundary between what it
  administers and what it denies. The abstraction doesn't decorate: it thinks.

## Where to go next

*Lo que vendrá* (1988), his previous feature: dystopian science fiction shot on the
margins of the same Buenos Aires, with Charly García on the soundtrack. I want to see
whether the gaze was already there —genre as the only possible language for a wounded
country— before the Möbius strip gave it its perfect form.
