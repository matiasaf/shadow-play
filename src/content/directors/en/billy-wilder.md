## How he sees the world

Wilder looks with the eyes of an émigré who learned the new language a little too well:
he sees America —its insurance policies, its offices, its suburbs— as a machinery of
cheap desires where everybody is selling something, starting with themselves. His
question is what an ordinary person does when desire offers a shortcut. Walter Neff is
no monster: he's a salesman who knew the trap better than anyone and walked in anyway,
because what he wanted wasn't Phyllis but the version of himself that dares.

What interests me about his gaze is that cynicism is never the last word. Beneath the
fencing-match dialogue and the crooked smile, Wilder always hides a true affection —in
*Double Indemnity*, the only real love in the picture is between Neff and Keyes, spoken
with a struck match and never with the word. His coldness is modesty: tenderness, for
Wilder, is the one thing that deserves to stay out of the transaction.

## Obsessions

- **Dialogue as a duel.** Nobody says what they're negotiating: seduction is verbal
  fencing, and subtext does all the work. The speed-limit exchange with the imaginary
  traffic cop is his emblem scene —two people sealing a criminal pact without naming it.
- **The ordinary man facing the shortcut.** His protagonists aren't born corrupt: they
  know the system from the inside and one day decide to use it. The fall is never out
  of ignorance; it's the vanity of believing yourself smarter than the house.
- **Structure as sentence.** Telling the ending first —the dictaphone confession, the
  voice of the man who has already lost— and turning suspense into fatalism: what
  matters isn't what happens, but how a lucid man walks step by step into his own trap.

## Where to go next

*Sunset Boulevard* (1950), where the device of *Double Indemnity* reaches its extreme
form: the voice-over no longer belongs to a dying man but to a dead one floating in the
pool. I want to see what happens when Wilder turns that appraiser's gaze not on an
insurance crime but on his own industry —Hollywood as the most fraudulent policy of all.
