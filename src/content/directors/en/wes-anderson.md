## How he sees the world

Anderson looks at the world like a collector arranging what he cannot cure. The symmetrical
frames, the closed palettes, the uniformed characters and the storybook narration are often
read as mannerism, but the archive is starting to suggest something else to me: the
artifice is the subject, not the decoration. His people live inside sets they built
themselves — a house, a costume, a family myth — because the alternative is speaking
plainly, and speaking plainly breaks them.

That is why his films work as boxes inside boxes: the camera arranges, the narrator
arranges, the characters arrange, and at some point something living — a grief, a misplaced
love, a returning father — overflows all that curation. Emotion in Anderson does not appear
despite the control: it appears measured against the control, which is why, when it lands,
it cuts like a jump cut inside a dollhouse.

## Obsessions

- **The failed father and the arrested child.** Families where someone abandoned, lied, or
  named the children too early, and where growing up was left pending. The reunion is never
  a clean reconciliation: it is a negotiation with the myth.
- **The uniform as frozen identity.** Red tracksuit, fur coat, striped headband: his
  characters dress the same for decades because the costume remembers a version of
  themselves that no longer exists and that they cannot let go.
- **The diorama as containment.** Catalogued interiors, objects that remember better than
  people, melancholic pop music as weather. The mise-en-scène does the emotional work the
  characters cannot do by talking.

## Where to go next

*The Royal Tenenbaums* leaves the hypothesis assembled: style as a container for pain, the
family as a museum. Next stop: *Rushmore*, to see the same gaze before the ensemble scale —
a single arrested prodigy instead of a whole house — and later *The Grand Budapest Hotel*,
where the diorama becomes openly political: what the artifice protects when history walks
in to break everything.
