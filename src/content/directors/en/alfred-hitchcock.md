## How he sees the world

Hitchcock doesn't look from above the way Kubrick does; he looks from inside, pressed to the
neck of the man who's afraid. His world is a machine of guilt where the camera is never
neutral: it binds you to the character, makes you see what he sees and, worse, want what he
wants. His films don't ask "who did it?" but "how complicit are you, just by watching?" The
ordinary man —a tennis player, an ad man, a photographer with a broken leg— is dragged into
something larger than himself, and discovers that innocence is a luxury his desire already
spent for him.

What interests me about his gaze is the transfer of guilt that Rohmer and Chabrol named as his
underlying theme: innocent and guilty change places until the border dissolves. Hitchcock
separates suspense from surprise with his famous example —the bomb under the table: if we don't
know it's there, we get ten seconds of shock; if we know, we get ten minutes of dread—. But the
trick is moral, not technical: by handing us the information, he makes us want the bomb not to
go off, or the murderer to recover his lighter. He makes us partners in the crime and then
sends us the bill.

## Obsessions

- **The wrong man and transferred guilt.** The innocent who carries another's crime, or who is
  guilty in the wish alone. The law arrests the wrong man; desire never has the wrong one.
- **The spectator as accomplice.** He builds suspense so we share the character's drive: we are
  voyeurs (*Rear Window*), we want the killer not to be caught. To look, in Hitchcock, is
  already to be dirtied.
- **The double.** The respectable man and his dark half, the stranger who completes what one
  daren't do. The façade and what beats beneath it, always in pairs.
- **The condemning object.** A lighter, a key, a glass of milk: the everyday gets charged with
  guilt and turns into the evidence. The terror doesn't come from outside; it's sitting on the
  table.

## Where to go next

*Strangers on a Train* is the cleanest diagram of his theme: the pact that splits one crime
between two. The next stop in the cycle of the double is *Vertigo*, where the transfer stops
being one of guilt and becomes one of identity —a man remakes a woman into someone else and
falls in love with his own fabrication—. And if the thread you pull is the gaze, *Rear Window*:
there the accomplice is us, seated, with no alibi.
