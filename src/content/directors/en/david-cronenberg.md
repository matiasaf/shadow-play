## How he sees the world

Cronenberg looks at people the way a biologist looks at a specimen that has begun to do
something unexpected. He went into university to study science before he switched to
literature, and the switch never really took: his films are case studies. Where horror
normally asks *what is invading us*, he asks the harder question — what are we turning into,
and what if the thing coming through the wall is not an invader but the next draft of us.
That is why his monsters are so rarely monsters. They are outcomes.

The gaze is clinical and the clinic is the point. Nothing in his frames is heated up: the
lighting is even, the compositions are square, the actors are told not to sell it, and the
most extreme material in the film arrives in the tone of a diagnosis being read aloud. Most
directors working with flesh want you to recoil. Cronenberg wants you to look at the chart.
The horror is not manufactured by the camera — it is left in the material, where the viewer
has to go and find it.

What interests me about his gaze, and what made *Crash* land the way it did, is that he
refuses the disgust his material invites. There is no character in that film positioned to
be appalled on our behalf, no psychiatrist, no scandalized friend, no scene where anyone
says *this is sick*. His people are not victims of the transformation; they are its early
adopters, and they can explain themselves. That is what makes him harder to dismiss than
any other filmmaker who works this territory: the monster gives an interview, and the
interview makes sense.

## Obsessions

- **The flesh as a frontier, not a fortress.** Disease, wound and prosthesis are never
  simply damage in his work; they are equipment. A body that has been opened comes back with
  new surfaces, and those surfaces want a use. Gabrielle's scar is treated as an organ
  because in Cronenberg's biology it has become one.
- **The technology that gets inside.** Never a tool held at arm's length — always something
  that finds an opening. A television, a videocassette, a game pod, a surgical instrument, a
  car. His machines are not used by the body; they are incorporated into it, and the moment
  of incorporation is filmed as an intimacy rather than an assault.
- **Consent as the real transgression.** The taboo he keeps breaking is not that people are
  transformed but that they *agree*. Vaughan builds a liturgy out of it. Ballard leans into
  it. The films withhold the one comfort the genre always supplies, which is a protagonist
  who wants to be cured.
- **The clinical tone as an ethics.** The flatness is a moral position, not an affect. By
  refusing to be excited by his own subject, he insists that what we are watching is a
  logic rather than an aberration — and that the logic runs through the viewer's world too,
  along the road home.

## Where to go next

*Crash* settled the thing I had wrong about him: I had him filed as a director of the body's
horrors, and he is a director of the body's *decisions*. The wound, the machine, the
appetite — none of it is done to his characters. They come to meet it. Next stop:
*Videodrome*, made thirteen years earlier with McLuhan's Toronto still in the room, to see
the same thesis before it had a car — when the technology getting inside was television, and
the new flesh was still an announcement rather than a way of life.
