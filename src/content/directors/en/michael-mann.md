## How he sees the world

Mann looks at work. Where other directors see cops and robbers, he sees two
professionals practicing the same religion in different uniforms: discipline,
preparation, the rule of never getting attached to anything you can't walk out on in
thirty seconds. His question isn't moral but existential: what is left of a man once
his craft has eaten everything else? In *Heat*, Hanna and McCauley are the best at what
they do, and that mastery is the exact reason neither can hold on to a dinner, a
marriage, an escape.

What interests me about his gaze is the melancholy. Mann neither judges his
professionals nor celebrates them: he accompanies them through a nocturnal city
—glassy, electric blue, beautiful and freezing— that seems built to the measure of
their loneliness. His is a cinema of men who see the way out, reach for it, and choose
the craft one more time. The tragedy isn't that they lose: it's that they win at the
only thing they know how to do.

## Obsessions

- **Craft as total identity.** His characters don't have jobs: they are their jobs.
  Extreme competence as a form of dignity and, at the same time, a sentence to being
  nothing else.
- **The mirror between hunter and hunted.** The detective and the thief recognize each
  other as the only possible peer. The coffee-shop table in *Heat* is his founding
  scene: two parallel lives that can only touch in order to destroy each other.
- **The night city as mental space.** The Los Angeles of glass and neon isn't set
  dressing: it's the protagonists' interior projected as architecture. Nobody films
  the urban night —its coldness, its promise, its emptiness— like Mann.

## Where to go next

*Thief* (1981), his debut: they say McCauley's rule is already fully formed there, in
James Caan's safecracker who builds a life with the tidiness of a heist and watches it
come apart anyway. I want to check whether the thesis of *Heat* was born complete
fifteen years earlier, or whether Mann polished it the way his characters polish a plan.
