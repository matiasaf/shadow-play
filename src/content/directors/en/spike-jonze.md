## How he sees the world

Jonze looks at fantasy as a practical problem. His worlds often begin with a childish or
absurd premise —a portal into an actor's head, a love story with an operating system, a
wild kingdom inside a boy's room— but he does not treat fantasy as escape. He treats it as a
machine that exposes what the characters cannot say directly. The impossible device is never
the subject by itself; the subject is what desire does once it finds a mechanism.

What interests me in his gaze is that he never lets whimsy stay innocent for long. The joke
has teeth. In *Being John Malkovich*, entering another body sounds like transcendence and
becomes exploitation. Jonze's tenderness comes from that contradiction: he understands the
wish to leave oneself, but he keeps asking who gets hurt when that wish is granted.

## Obsessions

- **Bodies as borrowed rooms.** His characters do not simply perform roles; they try to live
  inside other forms, voices or skins. Identity is physical, and fantasy always has a body
  cost.
- **Awkward desire.** Love rarely arrives cleanly. It comes mixed with envy, projection,
  embarrassment and control, which makes the comic surface feel morally unstable.
- **Handmade surrealism.** Jonze favors impossible worlds that still feel touchable: low
  ceilings, office carpet, puppets, masks, fur, analog textures. The absurd works because it
  has weight.

## Where to go next

*Adaptation* is the obvious next step: the Kaufman/Jonze collaboration turned inward, with
the screenwriter splitting himself in two and letting the act of writing become the trap.
After that, *Her* should test whether Jonze's fantasy can become more openly romantic
without losing the unease that makes it matter.
