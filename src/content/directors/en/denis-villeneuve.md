## How they see the world

Villeneuve builds his films around an asymmetry: someone competent, careful, well-trained,
versus a structure that does not notice them. The structure can be a colonial economy, an
intelligence apparatus, a species, a bloodline. His characters are never stupid—that is the
point—and their intelligence is precisely what lets them measure how little it will change.

What makes it cinema rather than thesis is that he stages that asymmetry as sensation. He
takes the object away from a stable reference: a desert with no foreground, a monolithic
building with no human scale, an aircraft in a landscape that offers nothing to compare it
against. The size lands in the body before it lands as an idea. Then he cuts to a face and
holds. That two-beat rhythm—overwhelming scale, then a person absorbing it—is his signature
sentence, and *Dune: Part One* is the most sustained version of it he has written.

The other half of his worldview is a distrust of the heroic. His protagonists tend to be
recruited: brought into a machine already running, told the rules late, and left to discover
that their role was assigned before they arrived. Paul Atreides is the extreme case, since
the very prophecy that flatters him was manufactured by the institution that bred him.
Villeneuve is at his best when he lets the audience feel the pull of the myth and then shows
the invoice.

## Obsessions

- **Scale as a moral instrument.** The sublime, used not to thrill but to establish that a
  character's will is not the biggest force in the frame.
- **Sound as pressure.** Voice, breath, low frequency and silence do the work melody usually
  does; the world presses on the ear before it speaks.
- **The recruited protagonist.** Someone brought into an operation whose real purpose is
  disclosed to them last, if at all.
- **Institutions that outlive intention.** Good people arriving inside bad structures and
  discovering that their decency does not alter the structure's function.
- **Prophecy, prediction, foreknowledge.** Characters who can see what is coming and cannot
  use it—knowledge as responsibility instead of power.

## Where to go next

The two *Dune* films are one argument in two movements, and the second is where it lands:
*Part One* assembles the machinery of the myth, *Part Two* watches a lucid man pick it up
anyway and gives the last word to the woman who won't kneel. Next stop: *Arrival* (2016),
the purest form of his obsession with foreknowledge—there, seeing
the future is a grief you accept rather than a war you unleash—and *Sicario* (2015) is the
recruited protagonist in its cruelest version, where the person we identify with turns out
not to be the film's subject at all.
