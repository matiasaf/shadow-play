## The central idea

That wonder is a calling, and every calling exacts a price. Roy Neary is no scientist or
hero: he's an Indiana electrician who sees a light on the road one night and comes back
branded. The film treats him as one of the chosen —the implanted vision of a mountain he
can't name— and Spielberg flips the entire genre with a single gesture: where fifties
science fiction saw invasion and paranoia, he films first contact as religious experience,
an annunciation in the suburbs. But the film is less naive than its reputation suggests:
the call destroys Roy's domestic life, and watching him board the ship is as luminous as
it is uncomfortable.

## Themes and ideas

- **Light as language.** The visitors never speak: they communicate with colors, with five
  notes, with music. The climax is literally a concert —a dialogue of tones and lights
  between the mountain and the ship. Spielberg proposes that contact needs no words, and
  defines his own medium along the way: cinema, too, is exactly that —light plus music.
- **Obsession as annunciation.** The mashed potatoes sculpted at the dinner table, the mud
  mountain in the living room, the "this means something" said on the verge of tears. The
  vision Roy doesn't understand makes him a madman to his family and a prophet to the
  film: Spielberg shoots vocation with the grammar of insanity and lets the two blur on
  purpose.
- **The State as curator of the miracle.** The government doesn't deny the visitors out of
  malice: it hides the truth in order to administer the encounter —the invented gas spill,
  the evacuated mountain, the chosen ones selected in uniform. The cover-up isn't
  repression but curatorship, and the film sets against it the ordinary man's right to be
  there.
- **The child's gaze.** Barry runs toward the light that paralyzes the adults. In the
  film's distribution of fear and wonder, Spielberg puts terror on the grown-ups' side and
  the smile on the child's: the thesis of his whole body of work —wonder is the original
  gaze; fear is learned— is already complete here.
- **The bookkeeping of wonder.** The scenes with Ronnie and the kids are shot like a
  horror film inside the wonder film: the family watching the father cry in the shower,
  the escape in the station wagon. Roy boards the ship and the film barely looks back —and
  that blind spot is now the most revealing thing to study: Spielberg himself later said
  he would never again have a father abandon his children.

## Screenplay and narrative

- **Three strands converging on a mountain.** The script braids Roy's domestic life,
  Lacombe's global investigation (the planes in the Sonoran desert, the chanting crowd in
  India) and Barry's abduction. Converging not on a piece of information but on an *image*
  —Devils Tower— is the most elegant decision: the characters don't share data, they share
  a shape.
- **The vision as narrative engine.** Roy doesn't investigate: he chases an image embedded
  in him that means nothing yet. The suspense isn't informational but iconographic —we
  know exactly what he knows: a silhouette— and the revelation (the mountain exists, it's
  in Wyoming) arrives through the TV, in precisely the right scene.
- **Revelation, rationed.** The ships appear first as lights, reflections, boiling clouds;
  the mothership only at the end, and then without stinginess. It's the lesson of *Jaws*
  applied to awe: the sublime is also cooked off-screen.
- **An ending that commits.** Roy boards. No epilogue, no return, no scene of the family
  reconciled. The script —the first of the two Spielberg ever signed alone— follows its
  faith to the final consequences, for better and for worse.

## Staging

Light is the true protagonist: Douglas Trumbull's effects show not objects but presences
—glows behind the clouds, oranges pouring through windows. And at the center of the
apparatus, a cinephile's gesture that is also a key: Lacombe is François Truffaut, a film
director cast as the only man who wants to *understand* rather than administer. The one
who listens, translates and watches with pure curiosity is, literally, a filmmaker:
Spielberg left inside the movie a statement about who should be in charge of wonder.

## The scene

Barry's abduction. Jillian's house assaulted by light: it comes in through the keyhole,
the vents, the chimney; screws unthread themselves; the mother runs to bolt doors and
windows while the boy, in the middle of the chaos, smiles. It's the most terrifying scene
in the film and it's built from the same materials as the scenes of wonder —the same light
that fascinates is the one that takes him. Spielberg holds both faces of the sublime at
once there, terror and awe without a border, and that ambiguity is the best thing in the
entire film.

## Philosophical notes

- **Rudolf Otto and the numinous.** In *The Idea of the Holy* (1917), Otto defines the
  sacred as *mysterium tremendum et fascinans*: that which terrifies and attracts in the
  same movement. The film is almost a demonstration of the concept —the same orange light
  that paralyzes the adults makes Barry smile, and the abduction and the communion are shot
  from identical materials. Spielberg doesn't choose between dread and fascination: he
  films the exact point where they are the same thing.
- **Kierkegaard and the knight of faith.** *Fear and Trembling* (1843) revolves around
  Abraham: the call that suspends the ethical, the faith that from the outside is
  indistinguishable from madness. Roy is a suburban knight of faith —he abandons his family
  for an order nobody else can verify, and the mashed potatoes are his domestic sacrifice
  of Isaac. The discomfort of the ending is textbook Kierkegaard: the film sides with the
  man who leapt, but cannot justify him to the ones left at the table.
- **Jung and UFOs as modern myth.** In *Flying Saucers: A Modern Myth of Things Seen in the
  Skies* (1958), Jung read the saucers not as ships but as projections of the collective
  unconscious: mandalas of light, symbols of wholeness for an age without religion. The
  film literalizes the thesis without quoting it: one image implanted in many minds at
  once, strangers converging on a mountain they dreamed separately, and a mothership that
  is, in visual terms, a giant mandala of light.
- **Weber and the re-enchantment of the world.** In "Science as a Vocation" (1919) Weber
  diagnosed modern disenchantment: a calculable world where the sacred no longer breaks
  through. Spielberg films exactly that irruption —the numinous returning to a world of
  power companies and military maps— but with a twist: here science isn't the enemy of
  enchantment but its translator. Lacombe and the five tones are rationalization placed in
  the service of liturgy.

## What I take

That the sublime can be rationed: show lights before ships, silhouettes before meanings,
and repay the wait with an ending that holds nothing back. And the question the film
leaves open in spite of itself: what is a calling worth if it demands abandoning the
family table? The film celebrates the chosen man who boards the ship; the ones left on the
ground are its debt, and *E.T.* —the encounter that arrives at the house of a boy with
separated parents— would be its amends.
