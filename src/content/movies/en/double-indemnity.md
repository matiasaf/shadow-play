## The central idea

That the verdict is already written from the first scene. Wilder opens with Walter Neff
wounded, confessing into a dictaphone: we know he has lost before he begins. The film
doesn't ask *what happens* but *how* a smart man walks into his own trap. The crime is
almost a pretext: what's studied is the machinery of desire and guilt.

## Themes and ideas

- **Fatalism as structure.** The flashback turns every decision into something already
  lost. "Straight down the line," the lovers keep repeating: the straight line isn't love,
  it's the streetcar track to the cemetery.
- **The femme fatale as a mirror of male desire.** Phyllis (Barbara Stanwyck) seduces not
  with mystery but with icy calculation; the ankle bracelet, the cheap blonde wig. Wilder
  makes it clear Neff doesn't fall for her: he falls for what he himself wanted to be.
- **The real romance is elsewhere.** The relationship that truly beats is Neff's with
  Keyes, the investigator. "I love you too," Keyes says at the end, lighting his cigarette.
  The tenderness is between the two men; with the woman there is only transaction.
- **Chiaroscuro as morality.** Venetian blinds stripe the faces, light leaks in as if
  through bars. John Seitz's photography doesn't decorate: it decrees that no one here
  stands on the side of the light.

## Screenplay and narrative

- **Confession as engine.** Wilder and Raymond Chandler's script uses Neff's voice-over as
  a noose he tightens on himself. Knowing the ending from the start doesn't drain the
  tension: it multiplies it, because we watch every step toward the abyss.
- **Keyes, the clock of the plot.** The investigator is both conscience and suspense
  engine: his "little man" in the stomach that signals when something doesn't add up. Each
  of his scenes tightens the trap without the killers seeing it coming.
- **Dialogue as fencing.** Chandler turns the seduction talk into a verbal duel —the famous
  "how fast was I going, officer?"—. The subtext does all the work: they never say what
  they're really negotiating.

## The scene

The murder we **don't** see. Wilder puts the camera on Phyllis's face as she drives, while
beside her, off-frame, Neff strangles the husband. That close-up —her faint smile, her
absolute control— says more than any shown violence. The horror is in what she feels, not
in what happens.

## What I take

That telling the ending first can be the most tense decision of all: suspense isn't *what*
but *how we fall*. And, as a way of seeing, that film noir isn't about crime but about
guilt —the light cut by the blinds is the soul of an entire genre.
