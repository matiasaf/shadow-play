## The central idea

That certainty is the real prisoner. Keller Dover (Hugh Jackman) is a carpenter, a
hunter, a believer, a man with a basement full of supplies who lives by his father's
rule: be ready. When his daughter and her friend disappear on Thanksgiving, he is ready
for everything except not knowing. Alex Jones (Paul Dano), a young man with the mind of a
child, is released for lack of evidence; Keller kidnaps him and starts beating out of
him a truth he has already decided. The film's question isn't who took the girls. It is
what a good man turns into when he cannot bear doubt.

## Themes and ideas

- **Preparedness as a theology.** The film opens with the Lord's Prayer over a deer
  hunt. Keller's faith and his survivalism are the same thing: a world where every threat
  can be anticipated if you are strong enough. When the threat cannot be seen, that
  strength has nowhere to go but into the nearest body.
- **The complicity of the decent.** Franklin and Nancy Birch, the other parents, know
  what is happening in the empty building. Nancy's line is the film's moral center: we
  won't help you, but we won't stop you. Villeneuve is less interested in the torturer
  than in the people who let the door stay closed.
- **The maze.** Alex's toy, the dead man's necklace, the drawings of the suspect Bob
  Taylor, the pattern on Loki's notebook: the film is full of labyrinths with no center.
  Every character is trapped in one, and the villain's plan, a "war on God", is itself a
  maze built to turn grieving parents into demons. Keller walks straight into it.
- **Two kinds of men.** Loki (Jake Gyllenhaal), the detective, has tattoos, a nervous
  blink and no visible life outside the case. He is method without faith; Keller is faith
  without method. Neither saves the girls cleanly, and the film refuses to rank them.

## Screenplay and narrative

- **A procedural that keeps going down.** Aaron Guzikowski's script follows the rules of
  the police thriller (suspects, false leads, a ticking clock) but each discovery takes
  us lower: a basement, a hidden room, a corpse under a priest's house, a pit under a car.
  The geography is vertical, and the truth is always underneath.
- **Parallel investigations.** Cross-cutting Loki's legal investigation with Keller's
  illegal one makes the viewer compare their yields, and the comparison is unsettling:
  the torture produces fragments that are half true, enough to tempt us into thinking it
  works.
- **The ending as a held breath.** The final scene cuts on a sound, the faint whistle
  from under the snow, and on Loki's face as he listens. No rescue is shown. The film
  ends on the one thing Keller never managed: waiting without knowing.

## The scene

The shower room. In the abandoned building he inherited, Keller nails plywood around a
bathroom, cuts a slot for the showerhead, and turns it into a box where he can rain
scalding or freezing water on Alex. Deakins lights it with grey daylight through the
boards; the walls are the same raw wood Keller works with every day. It is a craftsman's
cell, built with care. The horror isn't the violence, which is mostly offscreen, but the
care: the measured cuts, the practical solutions, a decent man applying his skills to
cruelty. When he walks out, he is the one who looks trapped.

## Philosophical notes

- **Kierkegaard and the suspension of the ethical.** In *Fear and Trembling* (1843),
  Abraham raises the knife over Isaac because faith asks him to step outside ethics.
  Keller makes the same move in reverse: he steps outside ethics *to protect* his child,
  certain that God and fatherhood both demand it. Kierkegaard insisted that such a leap is
  terrifying and unjustifiable from outside. The film agrees, and shows what it looks like
  from the outside: a bloody face in a plywood box.
- **Elaine Scarry and the voice of pain.** In *The Body in Pain* (1985), Scarry argues
  that torture doesn't extract truth; it destroys the victim's world and makes the
  torturer's version the only thing left to say. Alex says almost nothing useful, and what
  he does say Keller reads as confirmation. The man in the box can only echo the man
  outside it.
- **Michael Walzer and dirty hands.** In "Political Action: The Problem of Dirty Hands"
  (1973), Walzer asks whether someone may do wrong to prevent a greater wrong, and
  answers that he may, but must remain guilty. The film was released in the middle of the
  American debate on "enhanced interrogation", and Keller is its domestic version: the
  ticking-bomb argument moved into a suburban kitchen. Villeneuve gives him the
  punishment Walzer would ask for, a pit and a whistle.
- **Dostoevsky and the tears of a child.** In *The Brothers Karamazov* (1880), Ivan
  rejects any divine order that rests on the suffering of a single child. Holly Jones
  (Melissa Leo) has reached the same refusal and turned it into a crusade: if God allows
  children to die, she will take children to break other people's faith. She is Ivan's
  argument become monstrous.

## What I take

That a thriller can be a moral argument without losing its pulse: the suspense comes
from asking whether we want Keller to be right. And, as a way of seeing, that the most
frightening prison in the film isn't the pit or the box, but the certainty that lets a
man build them.
