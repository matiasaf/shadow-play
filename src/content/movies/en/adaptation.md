## The central idea

That you cannot adapt the world without being adapted by it. Charlie Kaufman is hired to turn
Susan Orlean's *The Orchid Thief* into a film, a book with no plot, no climax and no lesson,
only a woman who wishes she could care about anything the way John Laroche cares about orchids.
Charlie wants to be faithful to that drift. He wants a movie "about flowers," without car
chases, without people learning profound life lessons. He cannot write a page of it. So the
film becomes the record of that failure, and then, in a turn that is both a joke and a
confession, it becomes the very movie he swore he would never make.

The title is a pun that keeps opening. Adaptation is a literary operation, a biological law and
a psychological survival strategy. Orchids adapt to the one insect that can pollinate them;
Laroche adapts by dropping each obsession completely when it is done with him; Orlean adapts by
trying on another person's passion; Charlie refuses to adapt to anything, and the movie
punishes him by adapting for him.

## Themes and ideas

- **Passion as the thing you can't manufacture.** Laroche is ridiculous, toothless and brilliant,
  and he has what Orlean and Charlie lack: the capacity to want something completely. The film
  is less interested in orchids than in the envy of people who watch others love things.
- **The self as an unbearable narrator.** Charlie's voice-over is a long spiral of self-loathing,
  hunger, lust and procrastination. Kaufman makes interior monologue into slapstick: the mind
  trying to write becomes the main obstacle to writing.
- **The double as a split conscience.** Donald, the invented twin, is everything Charlie despises:
  cheerful, sociable, devoted to Robert McKee's seminar, writing a serial-killer thriller with a
  twist borrowed from a cop show. He is also happier, braver and more generous. The film never
  lets Charlie simply win the argument about art.
- **Hollywood as a theory of narrative.** McKee's rules (conflict, crisis, characters who change,
  never use voice-over, never cheat with a deus ex machina) hang over the movie like a
  superstition. The last act obeys them all with gleeful excess and, in doing so, shows what
  they cost and what they still manage to deliver.
- **Evolution as the frame.** The film opens its history at the beginning of life on Earth and
  keeps returning to Darwin. Kaufman stands in a long chain of adaptations, from cells to
  orchids to screenplays, and that cosmic scale makes his paralysis both funnier and more
  forgivable.

## Screenplay and narrative

- **Three timelines braided by failure.** Charlie in 1999–2000 struggling with the script,
  Orlean in 1994–95 reporting her book, and Laroche in the Fakahatchee swamp. The cross-cutting
  is not a puzzle but an argument: each strand comments on the others' inability to commit.
- **The script writes the script.** Charlie dictates into a recorder a scene in which he
  dictates into a recorder; he puts himself in the screenplay as a fat, bald, sweating
  screenwriter. The recursive gag is also a moral one: when you cannot face the world, you end
  up writing only about yourself.
- **The McKee hinge.** The seminar scene, where McKee demolishes Charlie's hope of a film in which
  nothing happens, is the pivot. After it, Donald takes over, and the movie turns into a thriller
  with drugs, sex, guns, a car crash and an alligator. The ending is either a betrayal of the book
  or the most honest adaptation possible of what happens when Hollywood touches it.
- **A credit for a man who doesn't exist.** The screenplay is credited to Charlie and Donald
  Kaufman, and the film is dedicated to Donald's memory. The fiction leaks into the credits; the
  joke becomes part of the object.

## The scene

Charlie and Donald hiding in the swamp at night, after escaping Orlean and Laroche. For the first
time Charlie asks his brother something real: how could Donald keep loving a girl in high school
who laughed at him behind his back? Donald answers that his love belonged to him, that she had no
right to it, and condenses it into a line Charlie will carry out of the film: "You are what you
love, not what loves you." Everything around this moment is genre machinery, gunfire, a crash,
an alligator. The scene itself is quiet, two identical faces in the mud, and it is the only point
where the hack teaches the artist something he could not learn alone. The cheap thriller ending
earns its sentimentality here.

## Philosophical notes

- **Darwin and the orchid.** In *On the Various Contrivances by which British and Foreign Orchids
  are Fertilised by Insects* (1862), Darwin used orchids to show how intricately organisms are
  shaped by their dependencies. The film borrows that wonder and turns it on people: everyone is
  shaped by what they need, and the ones who refuse to be shaped simply stop reproducing,
  creatively speaking.
- **Borges and the author who doubles.** In "Borges and I" (1960) the writer distinguishes the
  man who lives from the public name who writes, and admits he does not know which of them is
  writing the page. Charlie and Donald are that split staged as brothers: the man who suffers and
  the author who gets things done.
- **Harold Bloom and the anxiety of influence.** *The Anxiety of Influence* (1973) describes
  writers paralysed by precursors they must both obey and overcome. Charlie's precursors are
  Orlean's book and McKee's rules; he can neither be faithful to one nor free of the other, and
  the film is the record of that wrestling.
- **Schopenhauer and desire.** In *The World as Will and Representation* (1818), wanting is the
  engine of suffering. Orlean's longing to feel passion, and Charlie's longing for Amelia, are
  that will in miniature. Donald's line proposes the only way out the film allows: to own your
  wanting rather than wait for it to be returned.

## What I take

That the movie is wiser than its protagonist. Charlie believes the honest work is the one that
refuses compromise; the film suggests honesty might lie in admitting you compromised and showing
the seams. It is a comedy about paralysis that solves its own problem in public, and the
solution, a cheap ending told with total sincerity, turns out to be a small act of love toward
the twin he never had.
