## The central idea

That survival has its own minimal economy: pizza, beer, cigarettes. The title isn't local
color, it's a program —what these kids can desire without the desire crushing them. Caetano
and Stagnaro film the Buenos Aires of the neoliberal nineties from the sidewalk of those
left out of the model, with no explicit denunciation and no pity: exclusion isn't explained,
it's inhabited. The film founds New Argentine Cinema with one simple, brutal gesture:
letting the margins speak in their own language, with their own faces, at the height of
their own eyes.

## Themes and ideas

- **The city as a machine of exclusion.** Cordobés, Pablo and the rest move through the
  city center —the Obelisk, Corrientes avenue, the taxis— but never *belong* to it: they
  live off it as parasites. The camera follows them through a city built for other people;
  all they can do is steal pieces of it.
- **The hustle as a trade.** The robberies are small, clumsy, artisanal: the taxi scam, the
  snatch-and-run, begging outsourced to a disabled man. There is no criminal glamour; there
  is the day labor of desperation, with its own hierarchy (the man who pays always pays
  short).
- **Fatherhood as the only utopia.** Sandra is pregnant, and that gives Cordobés the only
  thing resembling a future: the boat to Uruguay, the fantasy of starting over across the
  river. The whole film is the distance between that shore and this dock.
- **Friendship as the only state.** Where there is no family, no school, no work, the crew
  is the institution that remains: it lends, betrays, forgives and stays. The loyalty
  between Cordobés and Pablo is the film's real social contract.

## Screenplay and narrative

- **Episodic structure, accumulated tension.** The script strings together loose hustles
  —almost documentary vignettes— and only near the end organizes itself into plot: the
  nightclub heist, the getaway, the dock. The drifting first hour isn't filler: it's the
  life the final job is trying to interrupt.
- **Language as territory.** The dialogue is in dense Buenos Aires slang, spoken by
  non-professional or barely professional actors (Héctor Anglada, Jorge Sesán), with dirty
  street sound. The script refuses to translate itself for a middle-class viewer: if the
  city won't listen to them, the film won't subtitle them either.
- **Information at ground level.** There is no backstory: we never learn where the kids
  come from or how they ended up here. The script hides the past because for them it
  doesn't count; only the present of the hustle exists and, barely, the minimal future of
  the boat.

## The scene

Inside the Obelisk. Cordobés and Pablo climb the monument's staircase and lean out of the
little windows at the top: for once they look at the city from above, from the exact center
of the symbol that excludes them. They conquer nothing —it's a clandestine visit, almost a
prank— but the shot states the film's whole program: put the invisible ones inside the
national monument and let them look. It's the only time Buenos Aires offers itself to them
whole, and it's borrowed.

## Philosophical notes

- **Zygmunt Bauman and wasted lives.** In *Wasted Lives* (2004), Bauman describes the
  "human waste" that modernization produces and no longer recycles: people the market
  doesn't need even as labor. The kids in the film are exactly that —not exploited but
  surplus— which is why their crime isn't rebellion: it's waste management performed by the
  waste itself.
- **Loïc Wacquant and advanced marginality.** *Urban Outcasts* (published in Spanish as
  *Parias urbanos*, 2001) studies the relegated neighborhoods where the state only shows up
  as police. The film shot it before it was written: no character ever meets another face
  of the state than the patrol car, and the "insecurity" the news denounces is, seen from
  below, a subsistence economy.
- **Beatriz Sarlo and the shop-window city.** In *Escenas de la vida posmoderna* (1994),
  Sarlo describes nineties Buenos Aires as a display case —shopping malls, consumption,
  surface— that expels whoever can't buy. Pizza, beer and cigarettes are the consumption of
  the expelled: the shop window seen from outside, reduced to its three affordable items.
- **Pasolini and sacred marginality.** *Accattone* (1961) and the *ragazzi di vita* are the
  direct grandparents of these kids: a camera that loves the bodies and faces of the
  lumpen without redeeming or condemning them. Caetano and Stagnaro inherit that pact: look
  straight at them, no moral attached.

## What I take

That realism is also a formal decision: non-professional faces, dirty sound and untranslated
slang say more than any speech about poverty. And that a title can be a thesis —three nouns
that measure the exact size of the desire the economic model left an entire generation.
