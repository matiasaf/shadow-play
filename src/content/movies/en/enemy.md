## The central idea

That the enemy is the part of yourself you have delegated to someone else. Adam, a
listless history teacher, sees an actor who looks exactly like him in a minor role in a
rented film, tracks him down, and finds Anthony: same face, same voice, same scar, a
pregnant wife, a motorcycle, a temper. The plot asks how two identical men can exist. The
film keeps answering a different question: what does a man need to invent so that he does
not have to choose between the life he has and the one he wants?

Read literally, *Enemy* is a thriller about a doppelgänger. Read the way Villeneuve builds
it, it is a portrait of one man split in two: the dull, guilty, solitary self and the
seductive, cruel, married self, each keeping the other's alibi. What looks like a mystery
about identity is really a study of avoidance, and avoidance is the most repetitive thing
there is.

## Themes and ideas

- **Repetition as a prison.** Adam teaches the same lecture twice: dictatorships control
  people through entertainment and censorship, and history repeats itself, the line that
  Marx borrowed from Hegel, "first as tragedy, then as farce." The camera shows him saying
  it again to another room, with the same gestures. The film's idea of the unconscious is
  exactly that: a script that runs whether or not anyone is watching. The question is not
  whether Adam will escape the pattern but whether he will even notice it.
- **The spider as the shape of a fear.** It appears at a private sex club, crushed under a
  stiletto; it hangs over Toronto like a monument; it returns in the final shot. Villeneuve
  never translates it, but the film's arrangement points to the women in Adam's life
  (mother, wife, lover) as the web he feels caught in. The spider is not a woman; it is how
  a man who wants no commitment sees commitment.
- **Totalitarianism of the self.** The epigraph says "Chaos is order yet undeciphered," and
  the lecture talks about regimes that control people by limiting what they can think. Adam
  lives under a small private regime: a routine that keeps him from thinking about himself.
  The city reinforces it—sickly yellow, high-rise towers, a tangle of tram cables that
  looks, in more than one shot, like a web.
- **The uncanny.** The double never feels like a fun twist; it feels like nausea. Gyllenhaal
  plays Adam and Anthony with small differences of posture and voice, enough to separate
  them and never enough to make them truly different. The horror is not that the other one
  exists. It is that he is so familiar.

## Screenplay and narrative

- **From Saramago to a nightmare.** Javier Gullón adapts *The Double* (*O Homem Duplicado*,
  2002), but strips out the novel's ironic narrator and its long reflections. What is left
  is a narrative stripped to its bones: two men, two women, a mother, a city. Saramago's
  sociological satire becomes a chamber of mirrors.
- **The clues hidden in the dialogue.** The film plays fair. Adam's mother (Isabella
  Rossellini) tells him to stop with this business of being a third-rate actor, and insists
  he has a good job, an apartment, a family that loves him. Helen, Anthony's wife, asks him
  if he is seeing *her* again. A pregnant wife, a past affair, a man who never stops being
  tempted: the information about one life is scattered across two men.
- **Structure as a loop.** The film opens with the key to the club and ends with the same
  key in an envelope. In between, Adam and Anthony exchange places, and the death of one
  frees the other to take his bed, his wife, his child on the way. But the final shot does
  not reward the switch: it starts the cycle again.
- **An ending that refuses explanation.** Adam, now in Anthony's apartment, finds the key and
  says he is going out that night. He goes to the bedroom to talk to Helen and finds a giant
  tarantula cowering against the wall. He does not scream. He sighs, as someone who
  recognizes a situation he has lived before. That sigh is the film's thesis.

## The scene

Anthony, wearing Adam's clothes and using his car, takes Mary to a hotel. He wants to sleep
with his double's girlfriend as revenge, or as conquest, or simply because he can. In bed,
Mary notices something on his finger: the pale band of skin where a wedding ring usually
sits. Adam does not wear a ring. She understands at once that the man in front of her is not
the one she knows, and the scene turns from seduction to panic. She demands that he take her
home.

It works because it is the only moment when someone outside the two men reads the truth
directly on a body. Everything else in the film is suggestion, rhyme and atmosphere; here
there is evidence, and it is the trace of a commitment that has been taken off but has not
disappeared. The ring is gone; the mark is there. That is the whole movie: you can remove
the sign of the life you have chosen, but your skin keeps the record. What follows, the
argument in the car and the crash, is the price of having been seen.

## Philosophical notes

- **José Saramago and the anonymous man.** In *The Double* (2002), Tertuliano Máximo Afonso
  is a man so ordinary that the existence of a copy feels like a theft of something he had
  never really used: his own uniqueness. Villeneuve keeps the premise and changes the
  tone. Where Saramago reads the double as a symptom of mass society, the film reads it as a
  symptom of a man who prefers to be two rather than to choose.
- **Fyodor Dostoevsky and the shameful double.** In *The Double* (1846), Golyadkin is
  displaced by a copy who is everything he would like to be and does not dare: charming,
  shameless, successful. Anthony is a version of that Golyadkin Jr. for Adam: the one who
  dares. The relationship is the same: envy of oneself, and terror at what one would do if
  one allowed it.
- **Sigmund Freud and the uncanny.** In "The Uncanny" (*Das Unheimliche*, 1919), Freud
  explains that what disturbs us most is not the strange but the familiar that comes back
  where it should not; he places the double among its main figures. The film's yellow,
  half-empty Toronto is uncanny in exactly that sense: it is a normal city that feels
  slightly wrong, like the face of a man who looks just like you.
- **Otto Rank and the double as defense.** In *The Double* (*Der Doppelgänger*, 1914), Rank
  describes the double first as insurance against death and then as the bearer of what the
  self repudiates. *Enemy* plays out both stages: Anthony is at first a fascinating
  possibility, and ends up being the place where Adam deposits his guilt, until Anthony
  dies and Adam simply occupies his life.
- **Karl Marx and the farce.** In *The Eighteenth Brumaire of Louis Bonaparte* (1852), Marx
  writes that history happens twice, first as tragedy and then as farce. Adam quotes it in
  class without hearing himself. His own story is the illustration: the affair that almost
  broke his marriage happened once, and when it is about to repeat, it repeats grotesquely.
- **Louise Bourgeois and the spider-mother.** The giant spiders inevitably evoke *Maman*
  (1999), the sculpture Bourgeois dedicated to her mother: a protector and a weaver, but
  also a trap. Villeneuve's spider carries that same ambiguity, and the scene with
  Rossellini's mother, controlling and tender at once, gives it a source.

## What I take

That a film can tell you everything and still not explain anything, and that its strength
lies there. *Enemy* is the smallest of Villeneuve's films and maybe the most personal in its
form: a puzzle that is not solved by finding the right answer, but by recognizing in the
final sigh a gesture that every viewer knows. Not the scream at the monster, but the
weariness of someone who has met it many times.
