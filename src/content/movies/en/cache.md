## The central idea

That the gaze is never neutral, and that whoever looks away is still responsible for what
they did not see. Georges Laurent (Daniel Auteuil) hosts a literary talk show; Anne
(Juliette Binoche) works in publishing; they live in a house with walls covered in books.
One morning a tape appears at their door: two hours of the front of their house, filmed
from across the street. Then come more tapes, and childish drawings of a face spitting
blood and a rooster with its throat cut. The plot asks who is sending them. The film answers
a different question: what did Georges do that he cannot bring himself to name? The answer
is Majid, the Algerian boy his parents nearly adopted in 1961, after the boy's parents
disappeared in the Paris massacre of 17 October. Georges, at six, lied to get rid of him.

## Themes and ideas

- **Surveillance without a source.** Haneke never reveals who made the tapes, and the
  question becomes less important the longer the film goes on. The camera that filmed the
  Laurents might be Majid's, his son's, Pierrot's, or simply the film's own. What matters is
  the effect: being watched forces Georges to remember, and he does everything possible not
  to.
- **Private guilt, public history.** Georges' lie as a child is the miniature of a national
  silence. October 1961, when the Paris police killed dozens of Algerian demonstrators and
  threw bodies into the Seine, was officially denied for decades. Haneke draws a straight line
  between a boy who sends another boy away and a country that prefers not to know what it
  did. Neither is ever put on trial.
- **The bourgeois fortress.** The Laurents' life is made of culture: the books, the TV set
  where Georges edits literary debates, the dinners with friends. It is a world that
  believes in conversation and understanding. Haneke shows that it can also be a machine for
  not listening. Georges talks constantly and says nothing.
- **Denial as a way of life.** Georges lies to Anne, lies to the police, lies to Majid's son,
  and finally lies to himself. When faced with the possibility that he did something wrong,
  his answer is never "what did I do?" but "what do they want from me?" The film is the
  portrait of a man who confuses being uncomfortable with being a victim.
- **The son as the next generation.** Pierrot, the Laurents' teenage son, disappears for a
  night and returns accusing his mother of having an affair. He is the one person who seems
  to see that something is rotten in the house. The final shot suggests the story is not
  over: the children may be the ones who have to deal with what the parents buried.

## Screenplay and narrative

- **The image you cannot trust.** The film opens with a long static shot of a street. Only
  when it fast-forwards do we realize that we are watching a tape alongside the characters. From
  then on, every fixed shot is suspect: is this the film, or another tape? Haneke turns the
  spectator into a participant in the surveillance.
- **The thriller with no solution.** The structure is that of a mystery: anonymous threats,
  clues, a suspect. But the solution never comes. Instead, the investigation reveals the
  investigator. It is a thriller turned inside out, where the guilty party is the one asking
  the questions.
- **Information through cuts.** The past appears in fragments: a bloodied boy in a dream, a
  conversation with Georges' mother, Majid's account. Haneke never gives a full flashback;
  the memory surfaces as Georges' mind lets it, which is to say reluctantly.
- **Violence without warning.** Majid's suicide happens in a single static shot, without
  music, without a cut. It lasts a second. It is one of the most shocking moments in modern
  cinema precisely because the camera does not prepare us or comfort us; it simply stays.
- **The final shot.** The steps of Pierrot's school, a crowd of students, a fixed camera. In
  a corner, almost invisible, Pierrot speaks to Majid's son. We do not hear what they say.
  Many viewers do not even notice. The film ends by asking whether we were watching.

## The scene

The film's first shot: a quiet street in Paris, the facade of a house, a car or two going
by, a cyclist. It lasts more than two minutes without moving. Over it, the
voices of Georges and Anne begin to talk; then the image freezes, stripes, and starts to
fast-forward. We have been watching a tape. Cut to the living room: the couple in front of the
TV, the books behind them, the cassette in the player.

It works because it shows the entire film in a single gesture. The shot is beautiful,
neutral, long, the kind of image we associate with art cinema. And it is a threat. Haneke
makes us realize that we were already looking at the Laurents the way their tormentor does,
and that we took pleasure in it. Later, the same setup returns with another tape, this time of
a tower block on the outskirts of the city, the address that will lead Georges to Majid.
The house the Laurents live in and the housing block where Majid lives are the two halves of
the same Paris. The camera has only to look at both to make the connection Georges refuses
to make.

## Philosophical notes

- **Michel Foucault and internalized surveillance.** In *Discipline and Punish* (1975),
  Foucault describes the panopticon: a prison where inmates cannot tell whether they are
  being watched, so they end up watching themselves. The tapes work exactly that way. No one
  needs to be behind the camera; the possibility is enough for Georges to start behaving
  like a man on trial, and the film never tells him the trial has an end.
- **Emmanuel Levinas and the face of the other.** In *Totality and Infinity* (1961),
  Levinas argues that ethics begins in the encounter with another person's face, which
  calls me to responsibility before I choose it. Majid's face is exactly what Georges cannot
  bear. When they finally meet, Majid is polite and devastated; Georges threatens him. The
  responsibility he rejects is not a theory, it is a man sitting in front of him.
- **Frantz Fanon and colonial violence.** In *The Wretched of the Earth* (1961), published
  the same year as the massacre, Fanon writes that colonialism is violence in its natural
  state, and that it shapes the colonizer as much as the colonized. Georges is not a
  policeman or a soldier, just a well-read man with a TV show. Haneke's point, very
  Fanonian, is that the colonial order does not need monsters; it needs comfortable people
  who never ask where their comfort comes from.
- **Sigmund Freud and screen memories.** In "Screen Memories" (1899), Freud explains that
  the childhood scenes we remember most vividly often cover up others we have repressed. When
  Georges tells his version of Majid, he remembers a sick, scary boy, a rooster, blood. What
  the memory hides is what he did: the lie that sent the boy away. The film is the slow
  failure of that screen.
- **Hannah Arendt and the banality of evasion.** In *Eichmann in Jerusalem* (1963), Arendt
  describes evil as a failure to think from the other's point of view. Georges is not a
  criminal, and his crime is small: a child's lie. But his adult refusal to reconsider it,
  even when a man's life depends on it, is the same failure on a domestic scale.

## What I take

That cinema can make you feel guilty for looking, and that this is not a trick but a
method. *Caché* never tells you who sent the tapes, because the question that matters is
another: what would you do if someone showed you the part of your life you chose not to see?
Georges goes to bed in the middle of the afternoon, closes the curtains, and takes two pills.
It is the most honest image of denial I know: not a lie, but a nap.
