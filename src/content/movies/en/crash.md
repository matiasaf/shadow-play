## The central idea

That technology has not merely changed what we do; it has rerouted what we want — and that
the rerouting is already complete, so there is no one left to warn. *Crash* is habitually
read as a provocation about perversion. It isn't. It is a film about people who have
noticed that their desire now runs through machinery, and who respond not with horror but
with method. They investigate. They compare notes. They go back out on the road.

That is why the flatness is not a flaw. The affectless line readings, the total absence of
a raised voice, the way a woman can describe a fatal collision in the tone of someone
reading a parts list — that is the sound of people who have stopped arguing with their own
condition. Every other film about a subculture gives you the outsider who is appalled.
Cronenberg deletes that character. There is nobody in *Crash* to be shocked on our behalf,
which leaves the job to us, and then quietly declines to reward us for doing it.

Vaughan states a thesis early: the reshaping of the human body by modern technology. Later
he retracts it, calling it a crude science-fiction concept that floats on the surface and
threatens nobody — something he uses to test the resilience of potential partners. What he
actually believes is stranger and closer to faith: a benevolent psychopathology beckoning
towards us. Cronenberg keeps both statements in the film. He knows the science-fiction
reading is the easy one, and he wants the other one, the one you cannot put on a poster.

## Themes and ideas

- **The wound as an organ.** The film's central invention is not the crash but the scar.
  Gabrielle's leg, ridged and split behind the thigh, is photographed and treated exactly as
  a genital, and when James goes down to it the film does not flinch, cut away, or wink.
  This is the idea the whole picture rests on: that trauma does not merely damage the body,
  it *equips* it. A crash survivor comes out of the wreck with new surfaces, and those
  surfaces demand a use. The braces, the pins, the fishnet stockings pulled over surgical
  steel — Cronenberg dresses the prosthetic as lingerie because in this world that is simply
  what it has become.
- **The car as the only remaining interior.** Every space in the film is transit
  architecture: parking structures, dealerships, hospital corridors, car washes, the
  balcony above an interchange, the shoulder of a road. Nobody has a living room. The one
  place these people are ever genuinely enclosed is inside a vehicle, which means the only
  privacy left in the film is a privacy travelling at speed toward something. The
  automobile is not a symbol here. It is the last room.
- **Sex without psychology.** There is no childhood, no trauma-origin, no explanatory
  confession anywhere in the picture. Nobody says *this started when*. Cronenberg strips out
  the motive layer entirely, and what remains is the astonishing spectacle of desire treated
  as a physical fact with no story attached — which is, of course, exactly how desire
  actually behaves, and exactly what films about sex normally spend their runtime denying.
- **Repetition as liturgy.** Vaughan's re-enactments — Dean's Porsche, Mansfield's Rolls —
  are not stunts and not nostalgia. They are rites, complete with a celebrant explaining the
  seating positions to a crowd. He is not commemorating those deaths; he is trying to make
  them happen again correctly, in front of witnesses, so that the event can be entered
  rather than remembered. When Seagrave dies performing the Mansfield crash, the group's
  reaction is not grief. It is something nearer to liturgical satisfaction.
- **Marriage as a research partnership.** The most easily missed thing about the Ballards is
  that they are not falling apart. James and Catherine sleep with other people and then
  describe it to each other in bed, in detail, as data — the reports are the sex. What
  begins as a couple who cannot reach each other becomes a couple with a shared project, and
  by the end they are the only two characters still working together. *Crash* is a film about
  a marriage that survives by finding a third party, and the third party is a machine.
- **Ballardian geography.** The film is shot in Toronto and never names it. Cronenberg
  drains every civic marker out of the frame until what is left is pure infrastructure —
  a city reduced to its circulatory system. These people do not live in a place. They live in
  a network, and their emotional weather is measured in traffic.

## Screenplay and narrative

- **An opening triptych that states the problem in three panels.** Catherine with the flight
  instructor against a plane in a hangar; James with an assistant in a film studio; then the
  two of them on the balcony, recounting it, failing. Three sex scenes in a row, all of them
  technically successful and all of them empty, before a word of plot arrives. The film
  hands you its premise as a fact rather than a development: this is a couple who can do
  everything and feel nothing, and the crash that follows is not what breaks them — it is
  the first thing in years that has worked.
- **A series, not an arc.** The structure is deliberately additive: each sequence introduces
  one new configuration of body, machine and injury, and the film moves on without escalating
  in the usual dramatic sense. There is no midpoint reversal, no ticking clock, no scene where
  someone decides. Cronenberg builds the picture the way a researcher builds a study — one
  variable at a time — and the effect is genuinely disorienting, because we have been trained
  to expect that a film accumulating incidents is heading somewhere it can be judged from.
- **Dialogue written as technical report.** The characters speak in specifications. Helen
  Remington delivers her most intimate lines with the diction of a doctor listing symptoms;
  Vaughan lectures. The screenplay lifts great slabs of Ballard's prose and then does the
  one thing that makes it playable: it refuses to let the actors interpret it. The
  performances are not underplayed. They are *unplayed*, which turns every line into an
  instrument reading.
- **The diagnostic scene that never comes.** Every conventional version of this story
  contains a psychiatrist, a police detective, a worried friend — someone positioned to name
  the pathology and thereby contain it. Cronenberg opens no such door. The one authority
  figure available, Dr. Remington, is a doctor who joins. The moment a character in this
  film could explain the group from outside, the group would become a case study, and the
  film would become safe.
- **An ending that opens instead of closing.** James runs Catherine's car off the road,
  finds her alive in the grass, and says: maybe the next one, darling. Maybe the next one.
  That is not resolution and it is not despair. It is a research protocol. The film stops,
  but the project doesn't, and Cronenberg's last gesture is to leave the door open on an
  experiment that can only conclude one way and is in no hurry to get there.

## Mise-en-scène

Peter Suschitzky shoots almost the entire film in a narrow band of blue-grey steel — night
sodium, overcast dusk, the aquarium light of parking garages — and lights skin so that it
reads as another finish, somewhere between upholstery and chrome. There is no warmth
anywhere in the palette until the very end, and even the grass on that embankment is a
sickly, drained green. Carol Spier's production design keeps every surface hard: laminate,
glass, painted metal, hospital enamel. The one soft thing in any frame is a body, and the
film keeps putting bodies against edges.

Howard Shore's score is the other half of the argument. Six electric guitars, three harps,
percussion — an ensemble designed to sound like a string orchestra that has been replaced,
part by part, with hardware. It shimmers where a romantic score would swell. It is,
precisely, erotic music played on machines, and it tells you what the film thinks about
its own subject long before anyone says it aloud.

## The scene

The last one, on the embankment. James has taken Vaughan's Lincoln — the 1963 Continental,
the Kennedy car, the film's most explicit piece of iconography and the only joke it makes —
and driven his wife off the road. Catherine has been thrown clear. The car lies on its side
in the grass with a wheel still turning. He comes down the slope to her, and the first thing
he does is check: *are you hurt?* She says she thinks she's all right. He begins to undress
her there, and says the film's last line: maybe the next one, darling. Maybe the next one.

Everything is in this. The tenderness is real — that is the part people miss, and the part
that makes the film unbearable rather than merely transgressive. He is not indifferent to
her survival; he is disappointed by it, and the disappointment is spoken with more affection
than anything else in the picture. *Crash* has spent a hundred minutes establishing that
these two cannot reach each other through any conventional channel, and here, finally, they
are in the same emotional place at the same time: two people lying in wet grass beside a
totalled car, in complete agreement about what would have to happen for them to feel
something.

Cronenberg holds it in a wide shot with the expressway still running behind them, its lights
coming on, its signage unreadable. He does not move in. The world that manufactured this
condition stays in frame for the whole scene, going about its business, and the last image
of the film is a marriage that has finally worked, on terms that guarantee it will kill them.

## Philosophical notes

- **Ballard and the death of affect.** In his 1974 introduction to the French edition of
  *Crash* (1973), Ballard names the diagnosis the film then dramatizes: the death of affect,
  a condition in which the endless mediation of catastrophe has burned out our capacity to
  respond to it, leaving violence available as sensation but not as meaning. He also calls
  the novel a cautionary tale and an extreme metaphor for an extreme situation — and
  Cronenberg's greatest fidelity is that he drops the caution and keeps the metaphor. The
  film does not warn. It reports, from inside, in the voice of the condition itself.
- **Freud and the death drive.** *Beyond the Pleasure Principle* (1920) proposes what
  Freud himself found difficult: a drive beyond pleasure, aimed at the restoration of an
  earlier, inanimate state, expressing itself as a compulsion to repeat. *Crash* is the
  most literal film ever made from that hypothesis. Eros and Thanatos are not opposed here,
  or even in tension — they are fused into a single circuit, and Vaughan's re-enactments are
  the repetition compulsion given a stage and an audience. Read alongside the *Three Essays*
  (1905), where the sexual drive is shown to be startlingly free of any fixed object, the
  film stops looking like an invention and starts looking like an extrapolation.
- **McLuhan and the extension that amputates.** *Understanding Media* (1964) argues that
  every technology is an extension of the human body — and, in the same movement, an
  autoamputation, a numbing of the function it takes over. Cronenberg studied in Toronto
  while McLuhan taught there and had already built *Videodrome* on his ideas; *Crash* is the
  sexual version of the same thesis. The car extends the body's reach and anaesthetizes its
  capacity to feel, and the crash is the only event violent enough to restore sensation to
  the amputated part. The wreck, in McLuhan's terms, is the nervous system reporting back.
- **Merleau-Ponty and the body schema.** *Phenomenology of Perception* (1945) describes how
  an instrument becomes part of the body that uses it: the blind man's cane stops being an
  object and becomes a zone of sensitivity, the driver comes to feel the width of the car as
  her own width. Cronenberg takes this exactly at its word. If the vehicle is genuinely
  incorporated into the body schema, then a collision is not an accident happening near you
  — it is something happening *to you*, in a part of yourself that happens to be made of
  steel. Everything erotic in the film follows from taking that sentence seriously.
- **Bataille and the breach of the discontinuous being.** *Erotism: Death and Sensuality*
  (1957) defines eroticism as assenting to life up to the point of death: we exist as sealed,
  discontinuous beings, and erotic experience is the violent momentary opening of that seal
  toward continuity. Bataille means the violation of a boundary, and he means it seriously
  enough that sacrifice and sex sit on the same page. Gabrielle's scar is that idea in one
  image — a body that has been opened by force and now offers the opening. No other film has
  taken Bataille this literally, and few have understood him this well.
- **Baudrillard and the wound as a sign.** Baudrillard's essay on Ballard's novel, collected
  in *Simulacra and Simulation* (1981), argues that *Crash* operates in a world where the
  body and technology no longer stand in a relation of use but have entered into pure
  circulation — no psychology, no depth, no meaning to the violence, only the exchange of
  marks on flesh. He is describing a book, but he could be describing Cronenberg's mise en
  scène shot for shot. Where I part company with Baudrillard is the ending: he sees a system
  with nothing inside it, and the last scene of this film contains something he leaves no
  room for, which is tenderness.

## What I take

That the most radical thing a film can do with a taboo is refuse to be excited by it.
Cronenberg had every incentive to make *Crash* lurid — the material is lurid, the scandal
around it was enormous, the Cannes jury handed it a special prize citing originality, daring
and audacity, and it was banned by a London council and shelved for months in the United
States. He made it clinical instead. The film's coldness is a formal argument: it insists
that this is not an aberration to be gawked at but a logic, and that the logic runs through
the reader's world too, along the same road they will drive home on.

And, as a way of seeing: *Crash* is the strongest case I know for the idea that a film's
tone can *be* its thesis. Nothing in the screenplay would survive a warmer performance
style; nothing in the images would survive a score with strings. Cronenberg found one
temperature and held it for a hundred minutes, and the discipline of that is what turns
material that could have been a provocation into something that behaves like a discovery.
