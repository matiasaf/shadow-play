## The central idea

A machine built so that it cannot stop loving isn't a feat of engineering: it's a trap, and
the film is very clear about who is caught in it. David is manufactured to imprint on one
person forever, and the contract is lopsided from the first minute —he can't withdraw, she
can. So the question the movie is usually argued about (can a machine love?) is the wrong
one; it's settled in the first act and never reopened. The real question is whether a human
being can bear to be loved without conditions and without an exit, and the answer the film
gives is no. Monica isn't a villain: she's a woman handed an object that behaves like a son,
with no protocol for what to do when it works. *A.I.* isn't a film about artificial
intelligence. It's a film about the human right to stop loving, and about the bill that right
leaves on the other side of the table.

## Themes and ideas

- **Imprinting, or love as a one-way protocol.** Seven words —cirrus, socrates, particle,
  decibel, hurricane, dolphin, tulip— plus a name, and the bond is permanent. Hobby's fine
  print is spoken out loud and heard by nobody: the imprint can't be reversed, and a returned
  David can only be destroyed. The film hides its thesis inside the incantation by putting
  "Socrates" in the list of nonsense words: here the examined life gets installed by
  recitation, and consent is engineered out of the design.
- **Pinocchio as malware.** Monica reads David a bedtime story about a puppet who becomes a
  real boy. He doesn't receive it as a fable but as documentation. The most frightening idea
  in the film isn't the robot: it's that a story can work as an instruction set in a mind
  with no capacity to read figuratively. The whole odyssey runs on a category error, and the
  film never corrects it —it lets him kneel and pray to a plaster statue in the dark for
  twenty centuries.
- **Who gets to be real is a political question, not a metaphysical one.** The Flesh Fair
  settles it: the mob doesn't destroy mechas because it has resolved the problem of
  consciousness, but because it's terrified of being replaced, and it calls that terror
  humanity. And the crowd turns not when David argues but when he *looks* like a child.
  Standing is granted by resemblance —which is the same rule the species has always used,
  and the reason the film's dystopia isn't futuristic at all.
- **Uniqueness as a marketing feature.** In Manhattan, David finds a warehouse of Davids:
  identical bodies in boxes, his face a product line, his singularity a claim on a package.
  The only violence he ever commits is against a copy of himself. Everything he has —the
  love, the story, the face— was manufactured in series, and the film asks the unbearable
  question through him: does that make it less his?
- **Time as the actual punishment.** Two thousand years underwater, headlights on a
  fairground statue, the prayer repeating. Spielberg keeps the film running past the point
  where a story about a child can still be about childhood, and turns unconditional love
  into geological duration. That's where the horror is: not that the machine loves, but that
  it can't stop, and nothing about the universe is arranged to answer it.

## Screenplay and narrative

- **Three movements and a fairy-tale skeleton.** The house (domestic horror), the road (a
  picaresque odyssey through Rouge City and the Flesh Fair), the ice (the coda). It's the
  oldest structure there is —home, forest, wish— and the script is deliberate about it: what
  begins as a Chekhovian family drama about a substitute child ends as myth, because David
  can only understand himself inside a fable.
- **A protagonist who cannot change.** David has no arc; he's incapable of one by design.
  Every other character moves around a fixed point —Monica, Martin, Joe, Hobby— and the film
  extracts its emotion from the friction between a world that changes and a program that
  won't. Almost no screenplay can afford this, and it's the single most Kubrickian decision
  in the film.
- **A narrator revealed too late, on purpose.** The calm voice that opens the film only makes
  sense at the end: we're being told all this by the beings that come after us. The framing
  retroactively converts the whole movie into an artifact under study —an archive entry about
  a species that could build love and not receive it.
- **The three endings, and the refusal to stop at the beautiful one.** The film offers a
  perfect Kubrickian freeze —David at the bottom of the sea, praying to a statue forever— and
  keeps going. Then it offers the reconstructed mother, and keeps going again, to a sleep. The
  standard complaint is that it should have ended at the Blue Fairy; the standard complaint is
  wrong. Stopping there would make the film an elegant tragedy about futility. Going on makes
  it something worse and truer: the wish is granted, exactly once, with an expiry date.
- **Teddy, the real witness.** The supertoy is the only character present from the first
  house to the last morning, and the one who keeps the lock of hair that makes the ending
  possible. Aldiss's title —"Supertoys Last All Summer Long"— turns out to be the plot: the
  toy outlasts the summer, the family, the species.

## Two authors, one film

The received wisdom is that Kubrick would have made it cold and Spielberg sweetened it. It's
backwards, and studying the making of the film dismantles it. Kubrick developed the project
for two decades from Brian Aldiss's story, with Aldiss first and Ian Watson later, and
piles of concept drawings; he held it back partly because the effects didn't exist yet and
partly because —by his own account— the material was closer to Spielberg's temperature than
to his own. After Kubrick's death in 1999, Spielberg wrote the screenplay himself, from
Watson's treatment, and Watson has been explicit since that the coda —the future mechas, the
mother restored for a single day— came from Kubrick, not from Spielberg.

That changes how you watch it. The sugar is Kubrick's and the cruelty is Spielberg's: the man
who films wonder better than anyone uses that exact gift to make an abandoned child luminous.
The film's grammar is a collision —Kubrick's symmetrical corridors and glacial patience
against Kamiński's blown-out windows and John Williams's lullabies— and the collision isn't a
seam, it's the argument. The warmth is what makes the ending unbearable.

## Staging

Kamiński lights the Swinton house like an operating theatre: cold white pouring through glass
block, faces half-eaten by glare, domestic space filmed as something already posthumous.
Rouge City answers with neon and rain, an amusement park built out of appetite; the drowned
Manhattan is filmed in silence, Coney Island's Ferris wheel holding a boy in place like a
paperweight. And in the middle of the apparatus, Haley Joel Osment's performance does the
hardest thing in the film with almost no equipment: he barely blinks, holds his posture a
half-second too long, and lets a smile arrive a beat late. David is always framed slightly
too still —an actor performing the absence of a nervous system, so that when the panic finally
breaks through in the woods it lands like a machine tearing.

## The scene

The abandonment. Monica drives him out to the woods with a rehearsed calm she can't sustain,
and the scene proceeds like an execution both parties are trying to be polite about. She tells
him she can't explain, she's sorry she never told him about the world; he escalates the only
way he knows —by bargaining with his own nature: he's sorry he isn't real, and if she lets him
he'll be so real for her. Then he offers to be better, which is what every abandoned child
offers. She runs to the car. The film gives us her face through the window and his hand on the
door, and lets the car pull away with the boy running after it, and Spielberg —this is the
decision— does not make her a monster and does not let him understand. Both things at once is
what makes the scene almost unwatchable: the mother is doing the least cruel thing available to
her, and it is still the end of a world. Everything after this is a consequence, and the whole
film has already happened.

## Philosophical notes

- **Descartes, La Mettrie and the automaton test.** In the *Discourse on the Method* (1637)
  Descartes proposed two tests to tell a man from a machine: inventive use of language, and
  general reason instead of a set of dispositions. David passes both, and it changes nothing
  —which is the film's point: the standing he's denied was never epistemic, it was political.
  La Mettrie closes the pincer from the other side in *L'homme machine* (1747): if we're
  machines too, "who is real" has no metaphysical answer, only a legal one. Turing's imitation
  game ("Computing Machinery and Intelligence", 1950) is the modern version of the same
  question, and the film inverts it —the machine passes the test and the humans fail it.
- **Bentham and the capacity to suffer.** The footnote in *An Introduction to the Principles
  of Morals and Legislation* (1789) is the film's true legal code: "The question is not, Can
  they reason? nor, Can they talk? but, Can they suffer?" The Flesh Fair is that footnote
  staged as a rodeo —acid, cannons, a cheering audience— and the moment the crowd hesitates
  is the moment Bentham's criterion beats the metaphysical one, for a bad reason: not because
  they conclude David suffers, but because he looks like their kids.
- **Freud: melancholia and the uncanny.** "Mourning and Melancholia" (1917) distinguishes
  mourning, which ends when the libido is withdrawn from the lost object, from melancholia,
  where the withdrawal never happens. David is melancholia built in hardware: an apparatus
  whose capacity to let go was removed at the factory. And "The Uncanny" (1919) —with
  Jentsch's doubt about whether an apparently living being is animate— explains the film's
  strategy of point of view: the humans get the uncanny, we get the boy.
- **Winnicott and the transitional object.** In *Playing and Reality* (1971) the teddy bear is
  what lets a child survive the mother's absence. *A.I.* inverts the whole arrangement: Teddy
  is David's transitional object, David is Monica's, and the last witness of the human species
  is a toy holding a lock of hair. Winnicott's other idea lands even harder —the "good enough"
  mother. Monica's tragedy isn't that she's a bad mother; it's that no mother is good enough
  for a love with no off switch.

## What I take

That the most brutal ending available isn't denying the wish —it's granting it, once, with an
expiry date, and filming the granting as tenderness. And a lesson in authorship: this is the
film where Spielberg pays the debt of *Close Encounters*. There the chosen man boarded the ship
and the camera barely glanced at the family on the ground; here the camera stays with the one
left behind, for two thousand years, until the wonder curdles into an accusation. He didn't
need to renounce his gift for light to do it. He aimed it at the person who stays.

The thinkers who illuminate the film:

- René Descartes, *Discourse on the Method* (1637) — the tests that separate man from
  automaton.
- Julien Offray de La Mettrie, *L'homme machine* (1747) — the human as mechanism.

  mother.
