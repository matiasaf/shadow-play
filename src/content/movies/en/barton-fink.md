## The central idea

That the worst enemy of the artist who claims to speak *for* the people is that he never
shuts up long enough to *hear* one. Barton Fink (John Turturro) arrives in 1941 Hollywood
fresh off a Broadway triumph, swollen with the gospel of "a new theater for the common
man." Then the common man moves in next door —Charlie Meadows, an insurance salesman with
a kind face and stories to tell— and Barton talks over every one of them. The film is a
horror movie about that deafness: the intellectual so in love with the *idea* of ordinary
people that the actual person beside him becomes invisible, until invisibility turns
murderous.

Underneath the comedy of Hollywood manners, the Coens build a closed nightmare about
creation, complicity, and a world sliding into war while the man of letters stares at his
own blank page. The blank page is the moral center: Barton can't write because he has
nothing to say to anyone he hasn't already imagined.

## Themes and ideas

- **"The life of the mind."** Barton's favorite phrase, the banner of his vocation —and the
  exact thing the film weaponizes against him. By the end Charlie screams it back at him
  while the hotel burns: the life of the mind, sealed off from everyone else's life, is a
  corridor on fire. The Coens treat intellectual self-regard as a kind of damnation.
- **The common man, unheard.** Charlie keeps saying "I could tell you stories," and Barton
  keeps cutting him off to deliver another speech about people like Charlie. The cruel joke
  is that Charlie is the richest story in the building and Barton, the great chronicler of
  the masses, can't be bothered to listen. His humanism is entirely theoretical.
- **The Hotel Earle as hell.** Peeling wallpaper that sweats glue, a heat that melts
  everything, a single bellhop rising out of the floor, the slogan "a day or a lifetime."
  The Coens film a purgatory disguised as a residential hotel —Barton has checked into his
  own conscience and can't get out.
- **Fascism at the edges.** The year is 1941. The detectives are named Mastrionotti and
  Deutsch —Italy and Germany. Charlie's real name is Karl "Madman" Mundt, and he runs down
  the burning hall howling "Heil Hitler." While Barton agonizes over his wrestling picture,
  the actual catastrophe of the century walks right past him. The film indicts the artist
  who mistakes his own block for the gravest problem in the world.
- **Hollywood as the dream factory that eats the writer.** Capitol Pictures, Jack Lipnick's
  bottomless flattery and sudden contempt, the order to produce "that Barton Fink feeling"
  on a wrestling B-picture. The studio wants the prestige of an artist and the obedience of
  a hack, and offers no exit from either.
- **The Jewish writer and the cultured monster.** Barton is a clear stand-in for Clifford
  Odets; W.P. Mayhew, for a sodden Faulkner. The Coens, writing a Jewish intellectual being
  ground down in 1941 while a man named Mundt shouts Hitler's name, let history press
  silently on the genre comedy.
- **The picture on the wall.** A woman on a beach, hand shading her eyes, looking at the
  sea. It is the only beauty in the room and the only thing Barton can't translate into
  words. When it comes literally true in the last shot, peace arrives as image, never as
  understanding —the serenity he could feel but never write.

## Screenplay and narrative

- **Realism abandoned by degrees.** The film starts as a sharp comedy of Hollywood
  pretension and slides, room by room, into surreal horror. The Coens never announce the
  turn; the wallpaper just keeps peeling until we're somewhere the rules no longer hold.
- **The mirror structure.** Barton is hired to write a wrestling picture —two bodies locked
  in a hold— and the film keeps doubling: writer and neighbor, art and pulp, the speech
  about the common man and the common man himself. Everything Barton theorizes about, the
  plot hands him in the flesh, and he misreads it every time.
- **The withheld box.** Charlie leaves Barton a package and tells him to mind it. We never
  learn what's inside, though the film makes us dread it. The Coens refuse the explanation a
  thriller would owe us: the box is whatever Barton won't open in himself.
- **Suspense from sound and texture.** The buzz of a mosquito, the gummy peel of wallpaper,
  the heat we can almost feel —dread is built from the room's materials before any violence
  arrives. The horror is atmospheric long before it's literal.
- **An ending that refuses to resolve.** Murder, a head perhaps in the box, a studio that
  owns him forever, a woman stepping out of a painting. The Coens close on enigma on
  purpose: a film about a writer who can't finish ends by refusing to finish for him.

## The scene

Charlie comes home. The hallway of the Hotel Earle is on fire —the walls themselves
burning in long sheets— and Karl Mundt walks straight down the inferno with a shotgun,
roaring "I'll show you the life of the mind!" as he guns down the two detectives. It is the
film's whole argument detonating at once: the phrase Barton wore like a halo turned into a
weapon, the genial neighbor revealed as the apocalypse, hell finally showing its face in
the building Barton mistook for a place to think. The corridor doesn't burn *around* the
idea —the idea *is* the fire.

## Echoes and film references

The film is a dense web of cinematic memory; the Coens build their nightmare out of other
people's movies as much as out of ideas.

- **Polanski's apartment horror.** The Hotel Earle is first cousin to *Repulsion* (1965) and
  above all *The Tenant* (1976): the room as a mind coming apart, the neighbor as menace, the
  walls that breathe. A neat circle —Roman Polanski headed the Cannes jury that handed *Barton
  Fink* its Palme d'Or.
- **Kubrick's *The Shining* (1980).** The other great movie about a blocked writer trapped in
  a haunted hotel: the endless corridor, the deserted lobby, the caretaker-author whose mind
  curdles into violence. Barton's Earle is the Overlook turned tenement.
- **Wilder's *Sunset Boulevard* (1950).** Hollywood as the machine that devours the writer.
  Where Wilder floats his narrator dead in a pool, the Coens seal theirs into a studio
  contract he can never escape —two films about the dream factory grinding down the dreamer.
- **Sturges's *Sullivan's Travels* (1941).** Released the very year the film is set, and its
  secret twin: a successful director who wants to make a serious picture about human suffering
  for "the common man." Sturges laughs the pretension off gently; the Coens let it end in
  fire.
- **The Hollywood the film parodies.** Jack Lipnick is a composite of the studio moguls —Louis
  B. Mayer, Harry Cohn, Jack Warner; W.P. Mayhew is William Faulkner sweating out scripts and
  bourbon on the studio lot; and Lipnick's demand for "a Wallace Beery wrestling picture"
  invokes the real MGM star of *The Champ* (1931). The B-picture Barton can't write is a genre
  that genuinely existed.

## Philosophical notes

- **Kafka and the inaccessible authority.** The Hotel Earle and Capitol Pictures are pure
  Kafka: a bureaucracy with infinite courtesy and no exit, an authority (Lipnick) who
  embraces and crushes you in the same breath. As in *The Castle* (1926) and *The Trial*
  (1925), the nightmare isn't punishment but the impossibility of ever understanding what is
  wanted of you, or of leaving the building where it's wanted.
- **Adorno, Horkheimer and the culture industry.** Barton's tragedy is the one diagnosed in
  *Dialectic of Enlightenment* (1947): the studio absorbs the rebel artist, sells his
  "feeling" as a product, and converts even the dream of art for the masses into another
  item on the assembly line. The film stages the culture industry literally swallowing the
  man who thought he'd write *against* it.
- **Sartre and *No Exit*.** *Huis clos* (1944) gives the hotel its logic: hell is a room you
  cannot leave and the other people in it. Charlie is Barton's hell precisely as another
  consciousness —someone who looks back, who has his own inner life Barton refuses to grant
  him. The sterility of the blank page is the Sartrean nausea of a freedom with nothing to
  say.
- **Arendt and the banality of evil.** Charlie is the friendly, complaining, utterly
  ordinary neighbor —and a serial killer working for the powers tearing the century apart.
  In the register of *Eichmann in Jerusalem* (1963), the monster wears the most common face
  of all, and the intellectual who claims to love the common man is the last to recognize
  evil when it brings him a drink and asks about his day.

## What I take

That a film can be a comedy, a Hollywood satire, a haunted-hotel horror and a fable about
fascism at once, and lose nothing by refusing to choose. And, as a way of seeing, the
hardest lesson it leaves me: that loving "humanity" in the abstract can be a way of never
having to listen to a single human being —that the life of the mind, sealed off from the
life next door, is just a hallway waiting to catch fire.
