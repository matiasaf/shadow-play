## The central idea

A gangster picture with a love story and a moral riddle hidden inside a maze of
double-crosses. Tom Reagan (Gabriel Byrne) is the adviser —the "brains"— to Leo (Albert
Finney), the Irish boss of an unnamed Prohibition city. When Leo refuses to hand the
chiseling bookie Bernie Bernbaum over to his rival Johnny Caspar, a war breaks out; and
Tom, who can see the whole board, sets himself against everyone —Leo, Caspar, Verna—
playing each faction off the others. The question the film keeps asking is *why*. Tom never
says.

The Coens build an entire movie around a man who acts decisively and refuses to explain
himself, so that loyalty and feeling can only be read off behavior, never speech. Under the
fedoras and the tommy guns, *Miller's Crossing* is a film about whether ethics is a code you
announce or a thing you do in the dark and never take credit for. Caspar opens the picture
with a speech about "ethics" and "character"; he is the most treacherous man in it. Tom
never once says the word; he is the only one who has any.

## Themes and ideas

- **The head and the heart.** Bernie's "Look into your heart!" is the film's hinge —the
  accusation that Tom is all calculation and no feeling. The whole plot is the slow proof
  that the opposite is true: Tom thinks for a living, but everything he actually *does* is
  governed by an attachment he will not confess, even to himself.
- **Ethics as talk versus ethics as act.** Johnny Caspar's opening monologue is a hymn to
  "friendship," "character," "ethics" —and he betrays every one of those words within the
  hour. The Coens stage the oldest moral irony there is: the man who lectures you on virtue
  is the one to watch, and real decency hides behind a sneer and a refusal to explain.
- **Loyalty without a name.** Tom takes beating after beating and never tells anyone whose
  side he's on, because saying it would cheapen it. "Nobody knows anybody —not that well,"
  Verna tells him, and the film both agrees and quietly refutes her: Tom knows exactly what
  he owes Leo, and would rather be hated than say so.
- **The hat, fate, and dignity.** Tom's recurring dream —the wind takes his hat and he
  chases it through the woods— is the film's emblem. "Nothing more foolish than a man chasing
  his hat," he says, refusing the symbol even as he lives inside it. The hat is the soul, the
  dignity, the thing that blows away the moment you grab for it.
- **Intelligence as a curse.** Tom out-thinks every man in the city and it buys him nothing
  but beatings and solitude. To see the whole board is to be unable to simply *belong* to one
  side of it. The smartest man in the room ends the film alone under the trees.
- **The unnamed city.** No real place, no real year beyond "Prohibition" —the Coens
  abstract the gangster world into a moral arena. The woods at Miller's Crossing are where
  men are taken to die and, therefore, the one place where the truth gets told.

## Screenplay and narrative

- **The labyrinth as design.** The plot is deliberately, almost impossibly intricate —who is
  conning whom shifts scene to scene— and that's the point. The film makes you feel what it
  is to be the one man holding all the threads at once. Tom's intelligence is the structure,
  not just a trait.
- **Withheld interiority.** We are never given Tom's reasons in voice-over or confession. A
  noir that refuses the confessing narrator: motive has to be inferred from a flinch, a lie,
  a gun fired into the air. The Coens trust the audience to read a man who won't be read.
- **The Hammett architecture.** The skeleton is Dashiell Hammett —*The Glass Key* (1931) for
  the adviser who loves his boss and takes the beatings, *Red Harvest* (1929) for the lone
  operator who sets rival gangs to destroy each other. The Coens write past pastiche into a
  fully realized world.
- **Dialogue as armor.** The clipped period slang —"What's the rumpus?", "the high hat,"
  "give him the bum's rush"— is a hard shell over feeling. Nobody in this film says what they
  mean; the style itself is a way of not looking into your heart.
- **The leitmotif that organizes everything.** The hat recurs —lost, found, blown away, set
  down in the leaves— until the dream and the clearing rhyme, and the title turns out to name
  both a place and a state of the soul.

## The scene

The walk into the woods. On Caspar's orders, Tom marches Bernie out to Miller's Crossing to
execute him. Bernie breaks —drops to his knees, crawling, weeping— and gives the film its
unforgettable plea: "I'm praying to you! Look in your heart! ... You can't kill me, Tom, I'm
*praying* to you!" Tom raises the pistol. And fires into the trees, and hisses at him to
disappear and never come back. It is the whole engine of the movie in one clearing: the man
of pure strategy performing an execution, and secretly committing the single merciful,
unstrategic act of his life —then having to spend the rest of the film as if he'd pulled the
trigger. The heart he's accused of not having decides everything, and he will never, ever
admit it.

## Echoes and sources

- **Dashiell Hammett.** The film is a loving fusion of two Hammett novels: *The Glass Key*
  (1931), whose Ned Beaumont is Tom's direct ancestor —the brilliant, loyal lieutenant who
  absorbs punishment for a boss he loves— and *Red Harvest* (1929), the template for the lone
  operator who plays warring factions against one another until they annihilate each other.
- **The gangster cycle.** The Coens build atop the 1930s Warner Bros. underworld —*The Public
  Enemy* (1931), *Scarface* (1932)— but drain it of rise-and-fall morality. There's no
  cautionary arc here; there's a chessboard.
- **The "Danny Boy" set piece.** Leo's one-man defense of his home, machine-gunning his
  attackers while the Irish ballad swells, is the Coens at their most operatic —violence
  staged as aria, a deliberate showpiece against the film's otherwise withholding cool.

## Philosophical notes

- **Aristotle and *philia*.** The bond between Tom and Leo is the *Nicomachean Ethics* (c.
  340 BCE) idea of friendship as the highest civic good —a loyalty that wants the other's
  good for the other's sake, not for advantage. Tom's refusal to *name* that loyalty is the
  film's deepest Aristotelian move: true *philia* needs no announcement, and the men who
  announce it (Caspar) have none.
- **Machiavelli and the angles.** Tom is the consigliere of *The Prince* (1532): the man who
  understands that power runs on appearances, that you keep your real intention hidden, that
  you sometimes do a brutal thing to prevent a worse one. The Coens give him Machiavellian
  *virtù* —and then ask what it costs a soul to live entirely as strategy.
- **Pascal and the reasons of the heart.** "The heart has its reasons of which reason knows
  nothing" (*Pensées*, 1670). Bernie's "look in your heart" is Pascal thrown in Tom's face.
  The film's wager is that Tom's deepest reasons are exactly the ones his formidable reason
  can't account for, and won't.
- **Kant and the unseen good act.** In the *Groundwork of the Metaphysics of Morals* (1785),
  an act has moral worth not for its reward or its witnesses but for being done from duty.
  Tom's mercy in the woods has worth *precisely because no one sees it* —he gains nothing,
  he's despised for the death everyone thinks he caused. It is goodness with the credit
  surgically removed.
- **Nietzsche and the mask.** "Every profound spirit needs a mask" (*Beyond Good and Evil*,
  1886). Tom is all mask —the sneer, the slang, the calculated lie— and the film suggests the
  mask is not the opposite of the man but the only form his decency can survive in.

## What I take

That a film can be cold all the way through and break your heart precisely *because* it never
asks you to feel —that withholding can be its own form of tenderness. *Miller's Crossing*
taught me to distrust everyone in a story who talks about ethics, and to watch instead for the
man who does the unglamorous, uncredited, merciful thing and then walks off so you can't thank
him. The hat blows away the instant he reaches for it. He keeps reaching anyway. That, the
film seems to say, is the whole of being a person with a heart you refuse to show.
