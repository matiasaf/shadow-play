## The central idea

That the worst prison isn't the room — it's the question. Oh Dae-su spends fifteen years
asking *why was I locked up?*, and when his captor frees him, the captor changes the
question: *not why I imprisoned you, but why I let you out.* Park Chan-wook builds the whole
film on that swap. Vengeance promises meaning, a clean line from wound to payment; the film
reveals that the line was drawn by the enemy, and walking it only completes his design. The
real subject isn't revenge but the **manufacture of a fate** — a man who thinks he is hunting
while he is being led by the nose to a horror engineered for him.

## Themes and ideas

- **Vengeance as a closed circuit.** Two men hollowed out by revenge, mirror images: Woo-jin
  spent his life building Dae-su's, and the moment the revenge is complete he has nothing left
  to live for. The film is merciless about this — getting what you wanted is the same as
  losing everything.
- **Memory as the soft place to attack.** Hypnosis, post-hypnotic suggestion, an implanted
  and erased past: Woo-jin doesn't just imprison Dae-su's body, he rewrites his mind. If we
  are the continuity of what we remember, then to edit a man's memory is to author his soul.
- **The Oedipal trap.** This is Sophocles in a Seoul high-rise: a man who, in seeking the
  truth of his own life, discovers he has committed the one unspeakable transgression — and
  that the discovery was the point. The investigation destroys the investigator.
- **Sympathy for the monster.** Park refuses easy villainy. Woo-jin's cruelty is grief turned
  into engineering; his sister's death is the wound under everything. The film asks whether a
  pain that vast earns the right to make another person suffer it back.

## Screenplay and narrative

- **The reframed question as engine.** The script (Park, with Hwang Jo-yun and Lim Joon-hyung,
  from Tsuchiya and Minegishi's manga) withholds not the *who* but the *why*, and then makes
  the why unbearable. The mystery structure is a delivery system for a moral detonation.
- **Anagnorisis as catastrophe.** Classical tragedy runs on recognition — the moment the hero
  sees what he has done. Oldboy times its recognition like a guillotine: the reveal doesn't
  resolve the plot, it annihilates the protagonist, who then begs to unknow what he has
  learned.
- **The tongue, not the eyes.** Where Oedipus blinds himself for having *seen*, Dae-su cuts
  out his tongue — punishing the part of him that *spoke*, that gossiped a secret into being
  decades ago. Park finds a new organ for an old self-mutilation, and it's exactly right.

## The scene

The corridor fight, in one unbroken side-scrolling take. Dae-su, hammer in hand, wades
through a hallway of thugs like a video-game level rendered in pain — no balletic grace, just
exhaustion, stumbling, a knife in the back he keeps fighting through. The single take refuses
to let us look away or cut to relief. It's the purest statement of the film's thesis: this is
what righteous fury actually looks like — ugly, depleting, and ultimately beside the point,
because the man pulling the strings is somewhere else, watching.

## Philosophical notes

- **Sophocles and the self-destroying inquiry.** *Oedipus Rex* (c. 429 BC) is the template:
  a man hunts the source of a curse only to find it is himself, and his triumph as
  investigator is his ruin as a person. Oldboy modernizes the structure exactly — including
  the unspeakable transgression revealed too late and the self-mutilation that answers it.
- **Aristotle and the architecture of recognition.** The *Poetics* (c. 335 BC) names the two
  engines of tragedy: *peripeteia* (reversal) and *anagnorisis* (recognition). Park engineers
  both to detonate at once; the film is almost a lab demonstration of why Aristotle thought
  recognition the most powerful device in drama.
- **Locke and memory as the seat of the self.** In the *Essay Concerning Human Understanding*
  (1689), Locke argues personal identity is the continuity of consciousness and memory, not
  the body. Woo-jin's hypnosis weaponizes exactly that claim: by editing what Dae-su can
  remember and what he is compelled to feel, he doesn't manipulate a man — he authors one.
- **Nietzsche and ressentiment.** *On the Genealogy of Morals* (1887) describes the soul
  poisoned by a vengeance it cannot release. Both Woo-jin and Dae-su are studies in it: lives
  organized entirely around a wound, so that completing the revenge leaves nothing but the
  void where the obsession used to be.

## What I take

That a mystery can be a moral weapon: Park spends two hours teaching us to crave an answer,
then makes the answer a punishment for having wanted it. And, as a way of seeing, that the
true horror of vengeance isn't the violence — it's discovering that your deepest feelings,
your love, your rage, were drafted by someone else, and that you were never the author of your
own life.
