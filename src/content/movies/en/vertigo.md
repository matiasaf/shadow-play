## The central idea

That love can be a kind of necromancy: not desire for a person but for an image, and the
refusal to let that image die. Scottie doesn't fall for Madeleine —he falls for a ghost, a
painting, a way of standing at a window with the light behind her— and when she dies he
sets about raising her from a living woman who happens to share her bones. *Vertigo* is the
only Hitchcock film that confesses the director's own crime: a man behind the controls,
molding a blonde into the shape of his obsession, willing to destroy her to make her perfect.

## Themes and ideas

- **The double as obsession.** Where the earlier doubles were another man (Bruno) or a
  partner in a pact (Phyllis), here the double is the dead self of one woman, and the horror
  is internal: Scottie wants Judy to *become* Madeleine, to be her own ghost. The cycle of
  the double ends where it was always heading —not in another person but in the impossible
  wish to make the lost one return.
- **The spiral as form.** The film is built out of circles: Saul Bass's whirlpools spinning
  out of an eye, Bernard Herrmann's score endlessly circling its own unresolved chord, the
  loop of hair in Madeleine's chignon, the coiled stairs of the bell tower. Hitchcock even
  invents a new shot for it —the dolly-zoom, track back while you zoom in— so that the floor
  seems to fall away while the walls stay put. Vertigo isn't fear of heights; it's the
  ground dissolving under a man who has lost his footing in the real.
- **The remade woman.** When Scottie dresses Judy, picks the gray suit, demands the blonde
  hair pulled back, he is doing on screen what every director does to an actress and what
  desire does to its object: erasing the person to install the fantasy. Laura Mulvey read
  this as the purest diagram of the male gaze —the film both performs the fetish and indicts
  it.
- **Guilt that needs no hands.** Scottie is Elster's patsy precisely because his acrophobia
  guarantees he can't climb the tower in time. He commits no murder; his fear does the work,
  the way Guy's wish did in *Strangers*. The transferred guilt of the whole cycle reaches
  its bleakest form: a man condemned by an incapacity, used as an alibi by the real killer.
- **Two deaths.** Madeleine dies twice —once as Elster's staged fall, once as Judy at the
  end— and it's the second that destroys Scottie, because the second is real and he caused
  it. The film argues that we don't mourn the dead; we mourn the version of them we built,
  and we will kill the living to keep it.

## Screenplay and narrative

- **The reveal that kills the trick.** Two-thirds in, Hitchcock does the unthinkable: he
  tells *us* —through Judy's flashback and letter— that she and Madeleine are the same woman
  and that Scottie was framed. The studio begged him to cut it. He refused, and he was right:
  suspense stops being "what is she?" and becomes the dread of watching Scottie reconstruct
  the very deception that broke him. It's the same engine as *Double Indemnity*: knowing the
  ending doesn't drain the tension, it makes every step unbearable.
- **A film in two rhyming halves.** The structure is itself a double. The first half is the
  pursuit of Madeleine; the second replays it as nightmare —the same museum, the same flowers,
  the same drive— with Scottie now the one doing the haunting. The second half is the first
  one dreamt again by a damaged man.
- **The necklace as proof.** As the lighter exposes Guy in *Strangers*, Carlotta's necklace
  exposes Judy: she clasps it on, Scottie sees it in the mirror, and the whole plot collapses
  in a single reflected object. Hitchcock lets the truth arrive not through dialogue but
  through a thing the obsessed man finally *sees*.

## The scene

The green kiss. Judy, remade at last into Madeleine, steps out of the bathroom bathed in the
sick green neon of the hotel sign, soft and unfocused like an apparition. Scottie kisses her,
the camera circles them in a slow 360°, and as it turns the hotel room dissolves into the
livery stable at San Juan Bautista —the place of the first death. The present is swallowed by
the past mid-embrace; he is kissing a ghost he manufactured, in the light of a corpse.
Hitchcock makes the most romantic image of the film also its most necrophiliac, and never
tells you which it is.

## Philosophical notes

- **Ovid, the Pygmalion myth (*Metamorphoses*, c. 8 AD).** The sculptor falls in love with
  the statue he carved and prays it to life. Scottie is Pygmalion in reverse and in despair:
  he carves a living woman back into a dead image. The myth grants Pygmalion his wish;
  Hitchcock shows what the wish costs when the clay has a heartbeat.
- **Orpheus and Eurydice.** The man permitted to lead the beloved out of death on the one
  condition that he not look —and who looks, and loses her twice. Scottie's whole second half
  is Orpheus refusing the condition: he must *see* Madeleine fully restored, and the seeing
  (the necklace, the tower) is exactly what kills her again.
- **Freud, *Mourning and Melancholia* (1917).** Mourning lets the lost object go; melancholia
  refuses, and keeps the dead alive inside the self at the cost of the living. Scottie is pure
  melancholia —unable to grieve Madeleine, he resurrects her in Judy and so can never love
  the woman actually in front of him. Add the *repetition compulsion* of *Beyond the Pleasure
  Principle* (1920): the spiral structure is the mind helplessly circling its trauma.
- **Slavoj Žižek, *Looking Awry* (1991).** Žižek reads Vertigo as the story of the "sublime
  object" —the fantasy frame that makes a person desirable— and of the "second death," the
  destruction not of the body but of the symbolic image we cling to. Judy must die a second
  time because what Scottie cannot survive is the collapse of the fantasy, not the loss of the
  woman.

## What I take

That this is where the cycle of the double had to end. Contract (*Strangers*), desire
(*Double Indemnity*), craft (*Heat*), and now obsession: the double stops being another body
in the world and becomes the impossible labor of making the lost one come back. And, as a way
of seeing, that *Vertigo* is Hitchcock turning the camera on his own desire —every remaking of
Judy is a directing, every directing a small murder of the real person for the sake of the
image. It's the most beautiful film about not being able to love what is actually there.
